Abstract



Privacy and compliance in blockchain systems face an inherent contradiction: privacy re-
quires that only the individual knows certain information, while compliance traditionally
requires revealing information to prove compliance criteria. This tension has led to two
unsatisfactory outcomes:

  1. Privacy systems without compliance attract illicit actors and face legal challenges.


                                   IE
  2. Compliance systems without privacy expose all transaction histories publicly, making



                                      W
     them unacceptable for consumer and commercial adoption.

Neither outcome supports the widespread adoption of blockchain technology, particularly




     PR
among traditional financial institutions where regulatory uncertainty and privacy concerns
are the top barriers to entry.
   We present a composable framework that resolves this conflict by enabling users to prove



       EV
compliance cryptographically without revealing private transaction data. In our system,
regulatory bodies publish compliance definitions as combinations of reusable logical primi-
tives called constraints. Applications select relevant compliance definitions based on their
regulatory obligations. Users generate zero-knowledge proofs demonstrating that they meet
these requirements, which are then verified on-chain before transactions execute. This pro-
vides proactive compliance: preventing non-compliant actions rather than detecting them
afterward, all while maintaining strong privacy guarantees.
   Our framework is distinguished by its composability and generality, designed explicitly
for a global ecosystem with many regulatory bodies whose requirements evolve over time.
Compliance definitions are chain-agnostic, modular with respect to privacy mechanisms,
versioned to accommodate regulatory updates, and can be combined arbitrarily which allows
applications to satisfy multiple jurisdictions simultaneously.

Below is relevant background information on blockchains, blockchain privacy systems, ZK

1.1      Zero-Knowledge Proof


                                                IE
Zero-knowledge (ZK) proofs are a cryptographic technique that allows a party to prove



                                                   W
specific claims about a computation without revealing any additional information about the
computation. The origins of this concept predate blockchain [7, 26], but it was described
elegantly and simply by Vitalik Buterin et al. in [13] as follows:



       PR
      The “claim” proven by a ZK proof is expressed as a type of program that is
      often called a circuit. Mathematically, it suffices to think of it as a function



         EV
      f (x, w) → {True, False}, where x is the public input, w is the private input, and
      f (·) is the function being computed. A ZK proof proves that, for a given x known
      both by the prover and the verifier, the prover knows a w such that f (x, w) returns
      True.

     SNARKs are a specific type of ZK proof that has the unique property of succinctness:
The proof size does not grow with the size of the computation, and the proof can be verified
very quickly relative to the computation [45].1
     A drawback with some SNARK schemes, such as Groth16 [27], is that they rely on a
trusted setup: a time-intensive ceremony involving multiple participants. A trusted setup
needs to be performed once per circuit [34] for Groth16 and some other SNARKs.
   1
     The SNARK acronym comes from Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge. The non-interactive term serves
to distinguish SNARKs from the interactive proofs between a prover and a verifier that are often used to illustrate the concept
of ZK for a general audience.




                                                              2
      A type of SNARK proofs, PLONK, mitigates this drawback by allowing trusted setups
to be re-used across circuits. With PLONK proofs, it is not necessary to initiate a new
trusted setup for every new circuit [25].


1.2       Compliance

Compliance is referred to as the goals and properties of a system to attempt to adhere
to a law or policy. Compliance is not immunity from prosecution, it is a best effort to
prove legality of actions. For example, US companies have systems in place to prevent
money laundering in order to be compliant with the US Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) - also
referred to as Anti-Money Laundering (AML) [61]. A typical effort to comply with AML
in the blockchain space is blocking addresses associated with sanctioned individuals from
interacting with a protocol.

                                                IE W
      A goal of this framework is to be flexible enough to accommodate compliance definitions
over all aspects of a transaction: the payer, the payee, the content of the transaction, and
even the actors’ transaction histories. Flexibility results in the ability to express any defini-




         PR
tion of compliance. This framework is built around the fact that definitions of compliance
vary over time and by jurisdiction.

          Blockchain Background

Blockchains are fundamentally data-storage systems with unique properties. Most impor-
tantly, data stored on a blockchain (on-chain) are immutable: history cannot be changed.
This property is achieved by allowing anyone around the world to participate in consen-
sus and validation of the blockchain’s state by running a node. There is a small class of
permissioned blockchains with access restrictions on who can use the chain or run a node.
      Nodes have a financial incentive to participate correctly in consensus. This mechanism
makes it economically infeasible for a single attacker to disrupt the network. For example,
in the Ethereum network, nodes who want to participate in block production must put
up a stake of the Ether currency2 which is “slashed” (lost forever) if the node behaves
incorrectly[52].
  2
      The original staking requirement was 32 Ether but it is subject to change.




                                                              3
   The first blockchain, Bitcoin, was created in 2008 and proposed a trustless ledger of
transaction histories[40]. Ethereum was created in 2013 and improved on Bitcoin by allowing
trustless execution of Turing-complete logic on-chain, referred to as smart contracts [11].
This allowed for more complex applications like marketplaces and lending platforms to exist
in a decentralized, permissionless, and immutable setting. These on-chain applications are
sometimes referred to as “decentralized applications” or dapps for short.
   Every transaction on a blockchain has a non-static monetary cost paid to the block-
producing node as an incentive to include the transaction in the next write. Some blockchains
with smart-contract support, like Ethereum, include an additional transaction cost driven
by the complexity of the smart-contract logic being executed.
   Users on blockchains are represented by a public-key address. The user signs messages



                                   IE
and transactions with the associated private key. Users can have multiple public-key ad-




                                      W
dresses. It is impossible to reveal who is associated with a public key unless the user’s
identity is revealed. Identity can be revealed directly by the user or indirectly by others
who know the public key’s identity, like a centralized exchange.




     PR
   A centralized exchange (CEX) is a company that allows users to trade traditional cur-
rency for cryptocurrency. They often require the user to connect their bank account and
supply regulatory documentation such as identification cards and proof of residency. They


       EV
collect this information to comply with “Know Your Customer” (KYC) laws. Using a CEX
is a common method for acquiring cryptocurrency. CEXs are custodial : they control the
address (they know the private key) that owns the cryptocurrency on-chain and allow the
user the ability to trade or transfer the cryptocurrency. They also allow users to send their
cryptocurrency from the CEX account to the users’ own on-chain address. CEX accounts
are not addresses. Rather, the CEX keeps its own addresses and the user’s holdings are
only an accounting record in the CEX’s books.
   A decentralized exchange (DEX) enables trading cryptocurrency while letting users re-
tain direct control of their holdings. In its simplest form, the DEX creates a smart-contract
to implement the trade and the users sign it with their private key.

1.4     Privacy in the Blockchain Setting

All data on blockchains are public by default unless the chain supports special privacy
features like Aztec Network [66] or Zcash [8]. There are also some enterprise permissioned
blockchains that restrict visibility levels. However, the vast majority of chains are public
by default, including Ethereum and Bitcoin.
   For public blockchains, every user’s entire transaction history is exposed to the world.
This is obviously undesirable for consumers. Most people do not want their purchase or
subscription history visible to the entire world. Many applications and protocols exist to
provide users varying degrees of privacy.
   Blockchain privacy protocols fall into two broad categories:



                                    IE
  • App-layer privacy: Applications on top of existing blockchains.




                                       W
  • Base-layer privacy: Entirely new blockchain architectures with privacy as a core iden-
      tity.




      PR
1.4.1     App-layer Privacy

CoinJoin was the first permissionless app-layer transaction mixer [36]. CoinJoin was built




        EV
on Bitcoin and combined multiple input Unspent Transaction Outputs (UTXOs) from dif-
ferent parties into multiple outputs, obscuring which inputs correspond to which outputs.
Tornado Cash was the first transaction mixer to use ZK proofs [44], and saw massive pop-
ularity [21]. ZK proofs are a common tool used to achieve privacy; ZK allows a party
to prove a computation without revealing the entire computation. See Section 1.1 for an
introduction to ZK proofs. The black-hat group Lazarus used Tornado Cash to aid in laun-
dering stolen funds and the Tornado Cash developers were put on trial [62]. Railgun [50]
and Hinkal [28] are transaction mixers that focus on compliance. Privacy Pools is a recent
transaction mixer that also focuses on compliance with association sets [13]. Ultra-Anon is
an experimental privacy token that has the property of plausible deniability: it isn’t revealed
if users utilized privacy [23]. Stealth addresses are ephemeral public keys that can receive
any tokens while hiding the receiver address [12]. zkBob is a wallet that has private transfer
for certain stablecoins [68]. Webb Protocol creates a cross-chain anonymity set [57]. Worm


                                              5
is an application implementation of “EIP-7503: Zero-Knowledge Wormholes” [32], which is
another transactional privacy solution with the property of plausible deniability.


1.4.2      Base-layer Privacy

Zcash is a privacy blockchain based on Bitcoin’s UTXO model. Zcash is implemented
from the 2014 Zerocash paper [8]. Monero is another private blockchain that leverages the
UTXO system and is based on the 2013 CryptoNote paper [63]. Grin is an implementation
of the 2016 Mimblewimble protocol, a modification of Bitcoin with private transfers [29].
Aztec is a general purpose layer 2 3 blockchain that allows for private state with smart-
contract support [66]. Aleo [2], based on the 2018 paper “ZEXE: Enabling Decentralized
Private Computation” [9], is also a blockchain with private state and smart contracts.



                                                 IE
The Miden blockchain supports the private state similarly [47]. Penumbra is a private




                                                    W
network and decentralized exchange for the Cosmos ecosystem [43]. Arc [35] and Tempo [58],
founded by Circle and Stripe, respectively, are stablecoin-focused blockchains with some
degree of transaction privacy. Payy is an Ethereum layer 2 with native transaction privacy




       PR
and compliance mechanisms [37]. Secret Network has private by default smart contract
state [54]. Ernst & Young (EY) released a permissioned layer 2 blockchain, Nightfall, with
private transactions and compliance features [24].


1.4.3
         EVCustodial Mixers

Custodial mixers are transaction privacy solutions that require users to send funds to a
trusted intermediary who then redistributes the funds. This accomplishes the privacy goal
of breaking the on-chain traceability between the input and output transactions, but cus-
todial mixers have the major weakness that operators could steal funds or keep records.
This is fundamentally weaker privacy because that intermediary has knowledge of a user’s
transaction history, rather than that history being private and held only by the user. While
this is not a legal document, we note that in some jurisdictions, the intermediary could be
subject to government subpoena.
    Blender.io was an example of a custodial mixer on Bitcoin where users sent funds to a
   3
     A layer 2 blockchain periodically syncs it state to another blockchain, referred to as the layer 1. In doing so, the layer 2
blockchain inherits the security and data guarantees of the layer 1.




                                                               6
trusted operator who then re-distributed the funds. Blender was sanctioned in 2022 by the
U.S. Treasury Department for failing to meet AML regulations [59].
   Some centralized exchanges can also be used as custodial mixers. A user deposits into the
centralized exchange from one address and withdraws from another address. From the on-
chain perspective, this looks like two unrelated transactions among the millions that occur
within that centralized exchange. However, the centralized exchange has full information
and knows the two addresses are owned by the same user.


1.4.4    Anonymity Set

An anonymity set is the group of entities among which an observer cannot reliably identify
which specific entity possesses a particular attribute or performed a particular action [46,



                                   IE
49]. Anonymity scales with set size: the larger the anonymity set, the less identifiable each
entity is [22].


                                      W
   For transaction mixer applications on blockchains like Railgun or Tornado Cash, any
address that has deposited can withdraw, but the link between depositing and withdrawing




      PR
addresses is never revealed. Each withdrawal could have come from any of the depositing
addresses. Therefore, the anonymity set of any given withdrawal from the mixer is the set
of all addresses that have deposited in the mixer. This description is oversimplified, and



        EV
in practice anonymity sets can be reduced with chain analysis [31, 38]. Anonymity here is
referring to the obfuscation of the link between a depositing and withdrawing address.

                       2.      Problem



                       Blockchains are fundamentally pseudonymous and public: user identities are obfuscated
                       behind addresses, but all activity between addresses is public by default. In practice,
                       entities are able to associate identities to addresses when on-ramping traditional funds
                       to cryptocurrency and therefore track individuals’ flows of funds on-chain. In order for




                                                                    IE
                       cryptocurrency to be practical for consumers, it must at the very least have the same level
                       of privacy as traditional finance, but ideally improves on traditional finance privacy. In



                                                                       W
                       traditional finance, typically two entities know information about a user’s funds; the user
                       and the exchange that is custodian of the funds. In blockchain settings, all entities can
                       track user funds and activity. Neither consumers nor corporations will want their entire



                              PR
                       trading, purchasing, or subscription histories publicly available.
                            Privacy is a growing field in the blockchain space [44, 32, 8, 23], but raises the concern that



                                EV
                       malicious actors may use privacy to launder funds. In an effort to reduce money laundering,
                       regulatory bodies are pursuing legal action against developers of privacy protocols [33, 62,
                       18]. This introduces the need to explicitly meet regulatory compliance inside private on-
                       chain applications.
                            The goal is to maximize privacy while maintaining the ability to prove compliance. Cur-
                       rent research efforts attempting to bridge privacy and compliance have proposed solutions
                       with strict domain limitations such as a new blockchain architecture, specific on-chain pro-
                       tocols, or privacy violations. We investigate a framework that allows for compliance in any
                       blockchain smart-contract environment and in any privacy protocol without compromising
                       privacy provided by the protocol.
